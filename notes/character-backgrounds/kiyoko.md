# Kiyoko

Proposed in play: Kiyoko can tell whose answer a meeting still needs. Experience gives her useful judgement and an occasional habit of deciding what a new person is like before they have finished speaking.

## Established

The `kiyoko` entry in [the bible](../../bible/facts.yaml) places her on the executive floors and establishes her approved appearance. [Art direction](../../art/PROMPTS.md) describes a woman in her fifties and uses 56 in a prompt; this set leaves exact biographical age unsettled. Her title is not established. Legacy links to Kaori or Mori are not current facts.

## Proposed history

Kiyoko's early work involved preparing senior meetings: locating the current version of a document, finding who could answer the remaining question and noticing when a decision had been described more confidently than it had been made. She became trusted because she could distinguish a genuine urgency from someone wanting to move their inconvenience onto another person.

Her present executive-floor role grows from that experience. She controls parts of the meeting and correspondence process, with the exact title to be settled later. She knows how influence operates without being omniscient. A familiar executive can still surprise her, and she can misjudge a new employee by remembering somebody superficially similar from years earlier.

In her childhood home, visitors were treated well and the household resumed being informal as soon as the door closed. She learned that dressing carefully and arranging a room could be pleasures in themselves. Her composure is partly enjoyment of doing those things well. She still likes changing the tablecloth when a friend comes to lunch, even if the meal is bought on the way home.

Her most durable friendship is with someone who changed employers and stopped caring about Amakawa's internal hierarchy. They have lunch when their schedules allow. Their latest disagreement concerns a novel told through three sisters' letters: Kiyoko liked the eldest sister and her friend found her unbearable. They also differ over whether a long restaurant queue can possibly be worthwhile. This friend occasionally tells Kiyoko that she has started giving instructions to the waiter before ordering. Kiyoko objects, then notices she has done it again.

An earlier long partnership included a small pleasure they made together: choosing a seaside inn from an old illustrated guide, taking a short holiday and adding an opinion in the margin when they got home. Kiyoko arranged the journeys; her partner chose detours once they arrived. She liked how much more they saw together. Later the partner began making separate plans and eventually ended the relationship. Kiyoko is still irritated by the claim that she made holidays feel like work, remembering a particularly happy trip she had planned down to the return train. She also knows she sometimes vetoed a detour because it spoiled her timing. She is currently unattached. The old inn guide recently turned up among her books. She has offered to return it and has marked one previously unvisited place on a separate sheet for a trip of her own.

## What this gives play

She recognises which person has not yet been heard in a meeting and can make room for them with one practical question. She can also assume her quick assessment saves time when it actually forecloses an answer. She enjoys being useful, likes thin blue-and-white porcelain plates with enough room for a slice of cake, and can be petty about a bad recommendation. She will eat from a chipped favourite plate at home while objecting to a restaurant's heavy new crockery.

She wants a recurring lunch kept free of company requests and to choose a new person for a practical responsibility on the strength of their work. The latter need not be the player. Her judgement should sometimes favour an existing colleague the player has barely noticed.

Incidental tells include moving a glass before a sleeve reaches it, remembering a junior employee's earlier suggestion, and reading the final pages of a book more slowly because she does not want to finish it.

## Discoveries across play

1. After her actual introduction and access are authored, a brief conversation about a delayed meeting can show what she considers urgent. No new access follows merely from a personal remark.
2. A later `yasumi` connection, grounded in something she actually said, can open whether the lunch she planned happened. She may have enjoyed it enough to forget a work question somebody wanted passed along.
3. If the player knows Saki, an agreed document handover can show their different forms of precision. Kiyoko asks who must act next; Saki asks what they have agreed to do. Neither must lose the exchange.
4. With established familiarity, Kiyoko can mention returning the inn guide. She may describe a breakfast room they both loved, then point out the inn she still wants to visit. A later conversation can recall that choice or return to their disagreement about the novel; she chooses how much of the old partnership to discuss.

Her personal history with Mori and Kaori remains undecided. Their legacy connections are not assumed by these encounters.
