# Creating memorable RPG characters

A useful character brief fits on one page. It tells us what someone is like and what they want. It explains why they might help the player or make life difficult. It gives us enough to write another encounter without inventing a new personality.

Amakawa needs people who are easy to recognise and different from one another. Some can be kind. Others can be selfish, abrasive, jealous or manipulative. They need reasons to spend time together despite those differences.

Dragon Age: Origins and the Mass Effect trilogy are the main references for this guide. The examples below contain spoilers. The character descriptions are my readings of the games. They are not BioWare's internal design documents.

## What the BioWare examples show

David Gaider describes companions who react differently to the same player. He also explains why a writer cannot assume what the player thinks. The character needs a clear personality. The player still needs room to answer. [Gaider interview, Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/interview-david-gaider/).

BioWare's party design also starts with the game's themes. Its writers consider what each companion thinks of the others. They do not expect the player to like everyone. This is described in interviews from Inquisition's development. It is useful evidence about their approach across Dragon Age. [Creating Dragon Age Party Members, Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/08/23/creating-dragon-age-party-members.aspx).

| Character | A short description | Where the depth comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Alistair | He is a friendly Grey Warden who often hides discomfort behind jokes. | His loyalty to Duncan and the Wardens limits what he will accept from the player. His friendly manner does not make every compromise acceptable. |
| Morrigan | She is a sharp, independent witch with little patience for conventional morality. | Friendship matters to her despite her distrust of dependence. Her interests can conflict with the player's. |
| Garrus | He wants to stop bad people and resents the rules that get in his way. | His pursuit of justice becomes entangled with anger and revenge. Shepard can challenge his methods. |
| Mordin | He is a brilliant, energetic scientist who cares for individual patients. | He also defends his work on the genophage modification. His compassion does not settle the moral argument. |

Alistair can leave over the decision to recruit Loghain. That gives his loyalty a cost the player can feel. [Alistair reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alistair_%28Dragon_Age%29). Gaider discusses friendship with Morrigan as a deliberate possibility. It need not become romance. [Gaider interview](https://www.killscreen.com/interview-david-gaider/).

Garrus's time in C-Sec develops into vigilantism and the pursuit of Sidonis. Those events put his convictions under different pressures. [Garrus reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrus_Vakarian). Mordin worked on a modification of the existing genophage. He did not invent the original. [Mordin reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordin_Solus).

These characters also have moments of pleasure. Mordin sings. Alistair jokes. A player can enjoy their company before facing a painful disagreement. John Dombrow describes making room for another Mordin song in his later material. [Dombrow interview, BioWare](https://blog.bioware.com/2012/04/02/interview-with-senior-writer-john-dombrow/).

The lesson for Amakawa is practical. Give people convictions that affect what they do. Let those convictions clash. Keep the qualities that make the player want to see them again.

## Design the cast together

Start by comparing the people who will share scenes. Different jobs and hobbies are not enough. Their desires and treatment of other people must differ too.

Use the four [current example bios](character-backgrounds/index.md) as the first test. They follow Jørgen's directions from 9 October.

| Person | What they want from other people | What can make them difficult |
|---|---|---|
| [Kuro](character-backgrounds/kuro.md) | She wants to be desired. She likes deciding where a date goes and how far it goes. | She can expect more attention than she returns. |
| [Mio](character-backgrounds/mio.md) | She wants most people to leave her alone. She makes exceptions for a few. | She can be dismissive and rude when someone needs her help. |
| [Rei](character-backgrounds/rei.md) | She wants people to follow her lead and do the job properly. | She can keep pushing a bad decision because she hates being wrong. |
| [Kenji](character-backgrounds/kenji.md) | He wants to help and understand what everyone expects. | He can agree to things he cannot manage. |

The first sentence of a bio should make this difference clear. The rest should make the person less predictable without erasing it.

Kuro and Rei can both be forceful. Their reasons differ. Kuro enjoys having the upper hand with someone she wants. Rei thinks her plan is the best one. Mio may refuse both of them. Kenji may agree to both and create a problem.

Kenji's kindness belongs in this cast. It gives the player a different kind of company. Strong contrast does not require everyone to become cruel or ambitious.

### Test them against the same situation

A proposed scene takes place after work. Kuro wants the player to swim with her. Rei needs a partner for tennis. Mio has already invited the player into a game. Kenji has been asked to help with equipment for both clubs.

Kuro expects her invitation to be tempting. Rei treats her plan as the sensible choice. Mio dislikes having a private arrangement turned into a group negotiation. Kenji is trying to work out whether he can do everything he promised.

The player cannot be everywhere. Their choice should affect the next meeting. A refusal need not end a friendship. It should still get a response from the person who was refused.

This is a small example of how the personalities can clash. Their larger stories can involve status, desire, rivalry, loyalty or control. The stakes should suit Amakawa's world.

## Give the character a strong reason to act

Ordinary interests help us get to know someone. They do not have to carry the whole story.

A wish to win can make Rei reckless. A wish to be wanted can make Kuro selfish. Mio's privacy can put her at odds with someone who depends on her. Kenji's wish to please can leave him helping the wrong person.

Ask what the character would risk to get what they want. Ask who might object. Ask what they would do if the player refused to help.

Some goals should be larger. A person might want a promotion badly enough to undermine a colleague. They might want revenge for a public humiliation. They might pursue someone who has chosen another partner. Someone else might risk their own position to protect a person they dislike.

Those are possible kinds of story. They are not new facts about the current cast. Choose a goal that fits the person and the setting. Do not give everyone a pleasant hobby and a problem that one considerate conversation can solve.

Amakawa is a company island with old machinery and understated fantasy. Its residents work together and meet again after work. That makes personal conflicts difficult to escape. Kotodama creates opportunities for unusual actions. Its origin stays unexplained under the [setting rules](../docs/game/setting.md).

## Write the one-page brief

About 250 to 450 words should usually be enough for a major recurring character. This is a working limit for this project. A minor character may need much less.

1. Describe the person in a few plain sentences. Say what spending time with them is like.
2. Say what they want. Include something that could make them act against another person's wishes.
3. Explain what they are good at. Give the player a reason to want their help or company.
4. Name a weakness and describe what they do because of it. Someone should bear the cost.
5. Include only the history that helps us understand their current behaviour. Leave the rest open.
6. Describe their relationships with two other characters. Give each person something they want from the other.
7. Explain how the player can get along with them or fall out with them.
8. Suggest a few different encounters. Keep the person recognisable across them.

A brief does not need a tragic past. It does not need a secret. It does need enough detail to help a writer decide what the character would actually do.

The Open University's character teaching discusses conflicting qualities and consequential choices. It also allows characters whose constancy matters more than change. [OpenLearn, Creating convincing characters](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=20435&printable=1).

### Replace vague traits with behaviour

| Weak description | A clearer description |
|---|---|
| She has difficulty with vulnerability. | She changes the subject when someone asks for an apology. |
| He struggles with confidence. | He agrees with the last person who spoke. |
| She has a complex relationship with authority. | She ignores instructions she thinks are stupid. She expects everyone to follow hers. |
| He is kind but flawed. | He covers for a friend and leaves someone else to take the blame. |
| She values her independence. | She refuses help even when she cannot finish the job alone. |

The clearer version gives us something to write. It also lets another person have a valid grievance.

## Use history sparingly

A past job can explain a skill. An old friendship can explain an exception to someone's usual behaviour. A bad decision can leave a grudge or a debt.

Keep a detail when it changes the present. Remove it when it only makes the biography longer.

For example, a former restaurant worker might notice that someone cannot afford another drink. That can affect the evening. The complete history of the restaurant probably will not.

Do not invent an ex-partner for every character. Do not make every past relationship teach the same lesson. Some people had happy relationships. Some behaved badly. Some still disagree about what happened.

Research real work before building a story around its details. A plausible anecdote is not evidence about Japanese office life.

## Give the player something to do

A character needs more than a conversation menu. Their personality should affect an activity the player can join.

Mio might invite the player into a difficult game. Rei might insist on a doubles tactic that is failing. Kuro might turn a swimming invitation into a dare. Kenji might need help with a task he has misunderstood.

Each activity needs a clear situation. The player should know what they can try. The result should be visible.

### A short Kenji example

This is an English meaning sketch. Kenji would speak Japanese in the game.

> Kenji is standing outside the gym with two bags of equipment.
>
> "I said I'd help Rei," he says. "Then Kuro asked me too."
>
> "Did you tell her you were helping Rei?"
>
> "I thought I could do both."
>
> He looks towards the pool.
>
> "They both said six."

The player can carry one bag, help him explain or leave him to sort it out. Carrying a bag solves the immediate problem. It does not settle the two promises. Someone still needs to know he cannot be in both places.

The scene can be funny without making Kenji a fool. He has made an understandable mistake. His fear of disappointing someone has made it worse.

### A Rei example with consequences

Rei insists that her partner cover one side of the court. The pair keep losing points there. The partner wants to change their positions.

| Player's choice | Possible result |
|---|---|
| Follow Rei's plan. | She gets the cooperation she wanted. They still have to deal with the result. |
| Challenge the plan. | She argues. Trying the alternative may expose her mistake. |
| Let her blame the partner. | The argument stops for now. The partner has a reason to resent both of them. |
| Leave the match. | Rei loses a partner. The next conversation starts with that disagreement. |

The writer must decide what actually works on court. Rei should sometimes be right. Her abrasiveness is more interesting when the player cannot dismiss every judgement she makes.

### Let the fantasy matter

An old machine obeys the player's Japanese command in an awkwardly literal way. Everyone present must already know about kotodama before they discuss it freely.

Mio wants to know exactly what was said. Rei wants the problem fixed now. Kenji is trying to remember whether he repeated the same instruction. Their personalities affect the advice the player receives.

The player can try a correction or leave the result alone. The machine's response must be visible. The scene needs no explanation for the power's origin.

## Write relationships that can change without making everyone alike

A closer Mio should still want time alone. Rei can respect the player and remain blunt. Kuro can become attached without losing her desire to take charge. Kenji can learn to refuse a request and remain mild.

Ask what changes between the two people. Perhaps one starts asking for help. Perhaps an invitation becomes more personal. Perhaps they stop pretending to agree.

A disagreement can reveal more than a confession. Banter between two characters can show a side the player has never seen. Gaider describes using companion banter to reveal attitudes and relationships. [Gaider interview](https://www.killscreen.com/interview-david-gaider/).

Writing Excuses offers useful questions about money, manners, morals and humour. Differences in those areas can explain why people argue. Shared interests can explain why they remain close. [Character Relationships](https://writingexcuses.com/13-26-character-relationships/).

## Make the dialogue understandable

Amakawa's player starts with very little Japanese. A character's English level cannot change because a scene needs a confession.

Use known words, gestures and visible actions in early encounters. A character can point towards the pool and wait for an answer. A complicated argument needs more shared language or help from someone who can translate.

Keep the established [voice notes](../game3d/story/VOICE.md). Mio's English should stay loose and natural. Kenji's English should stay limited. Kuro can flirt directly without giving a long speech.

Subtext needs context. A remark about an empty chair means little until the player knows who was supposed to sit there. [Writing Excuses, Developing Subtext](https://writingexcuses.com/17-34-developing-subtext/).

## Check whether the brief will last

Try writing an encounter at work and another outside it. Include a disagreement. Include a moment the character enjoys. Give them something to do with another NPC.

Then try a player who declines. The next encounter must still make sense. Characters can be disappointed or angry without every refusal ending their story.

Keep only the memories that change a later encounter. An accepted invitation might change a greeting. A broken promise might change whether someone asks again. Several branches can return to the same place with different expectations. Gaider discusses bringing dialogue branches back together to keep the writing manageable. [Gaider interview](https://www.killscreen.com/interview-david-gaider/).

Ask a reader to describe each character from memory. Ask which two are easiest to confuse. Ask what each would do with a favour the player owed them. If the answers are nearly identical, revise the cast before adding more history.
